1. The assignment

One complete, publication-ready image-led post for Instagram and Facebook, aimed at a US audience. No monthly calendar. This is Post 001 and it must establish the studio's standard.

2. The angle

A steamship capsized while still tied to its dock, killing 844 people in twenty feet of water, twenty feet from shore — and nobody was ever held responsible.

3. Why this story, why now

  • The central fact is a contradiction strong enough to stop a scroll: the ship never left the dock.
  • It is a genuine engineering-failure story: top-weight, stability, and a safety law that made a

known flaw worse. That makes it relevant to modern readers, not just history buffs.

  • Primary sources are abundant and free: NARA, the Library of Congress, congressional records.
  • It is widely under-known relative to its scale — 844 deaths, the deadliest wreck in Great Lakes

history — which gives it "I never knew this" share value.

  • Authentic public-domain photography exists, so no AI reconstruction is needed and no disclosure

is required.

4. Non-negotiable facts

These are verified before writing and may not change without a new fact-check:

  • The Eastland capsized on July 24, 1915, in the Chicago River, while still moored to the wharf.
  • 844 passengers and crew died.
  • The water at the site was about 20 feet deep; the ship lay roughly 20 feet from the dock wall.
  • The ship had a documented history of severe listing before the disaster.
  • The dead included a large number of young people and multiple entire families.
  • Criminal prosecutions ended without a conviction.
  • The ship was later raised, renamed USS Wilmette, and served into the 1940s.

5. Audience

US adults 25–55 who follow history, engineering failures, and true-story accounts on Instagram and Facebook. They save and share posts that teach them something specific they did not know. They dislike being manipulated and they notice errors.

  • Facebook: 4:5, 1080×1350, platform-native caption, 3–4 hashtags.
  • Image count: six. Determined by the Art Director after the outline (see 08-visual-plan.md).

Research — The Ship That Never Left the Dock

Slug: first-post

Journalist: research-journalist

Status: DRAFT → awaiting G2 factual lock

Last updated: 2026-10-19

Claim IDs refer to 03-source-ledger.md.

1. The story in one paragraph

On the morning of July 24, 1915, roughly 2,500 Western Electric employees and their families boarded the excursion steamer Eastland at a wharf on the Chicago River for a company picnic in Michigan City, Indiana. The ship had a documented history of severe listing. New lifeboat requirements, added after the Titanic, had placed additional weight high on an already top-heavy hull. Shortly after 7 a.m., with the gangplank still down and the ship still tied to the dock, the Eastland rolled onto its side. Because the river was only about 20 feet deep and the ship lay only about 20 feet from the wharf, hundreds were able to escape across the exposed hull — but 844 people died, most of them below decks. Prosecutions followed and failed. The ship was raised, sold to the Navy, renamed USS Wilmette, and served into the 1940s.

4. The mechanism

Three factors combined. This is the engineering core of the story.

Design and history. The Eastland was built narrow and fast, which tends to make a hull top-heavy. Its stability was marginal from the start, and it had a documented record of severe listing — in 1904, 1906, and 1912 — before the disaster (C10, C12).

Added top-weight. After the Titanic, the Seamen's Act of 1915 required more lifeboats and rafts. To keep its licensed passenger capacity, the Eastland's operators added boats high on the superstructure. That weight sat above the ship's center of gravity and worsened the very problem that had nearly sunk the ship before (C11).

The morning it happened. Passengers gathered on the side of the ship away from the river as it listed and corrected. When it rolled, the open lower decks and portholes allowed water in quickly, and the ship went onto its side within a short time (C03).

Why so many died, and why so many lived. The Eastland settled into roughly 20 feet of water about 20 feet from the wharf. The shallow water and the proximity of the dock saved hundreds, who climbed out across the exposed hull. It also meant the lower decks flooded within minutes, killing the people trapped there — which is why the death toll was so high despite the ship never leaving the dock (C04, C05, C06).

5. What was done afterward

A coroner's inquest and a grand jury investigated. The grand jury indicted the ship's owners, the captain, the chief engineer, and federal inspectors. The case was moved to Grand Rapids, Michigan, where the defendants were found not guilty in February 1916, and the court refused extradition to Illinois — so no criminal trial was ever held in Chicago. No one was criminally convicted. The civil claims dragged on for years and ended with liability limited to the wreck's salvage value; the families ultimately received no damages (C12). The ship itself was raised on August 14, 1915, sold, and later commissioned as the Navy's USS Wilmette, serving into the 1940s before being scrapped (C13, C14).

7. What we still do not know

  • The precise second-by-second sequence aboard the lower decks is not recoverable.
  • Sources differ on the exact time the ship rolled; we say "shortly after 7 a.m." to stay safe.
  • The number of crew among the dead is genuinely inconsistent across reputable sources.

8. Quotations available for use

We could not verify any quotation word-for-word from a primary source with a stable citation. No direct quotations will be used. Everything is paraphrased.

9. Notes on sourcing quality

  • NARA and the Library of Congress provide authoritative primary imagery.
  • The congressional record provides the age and family statistics, which is a strong source tier.
  • Popular retellings of the disaster frequently repeat the unfounded "never should have been built"

line and the "more than the Titanic" comparison. Neither will appear unqualified.

  • Wikipedia was used only as a map to better sources, never as a final citation.

2. The material errors found

Error 1 — a fabricated name. The draft named "Paul Steinbrecher" as a defendant whose extradition was refused. No person by that name is connected to this case in any source consulted. The six men indicted were Arnold, Hull, Reid, Eckliff, Pedersen, and Erickson. Removed.

Error 2 — wrongly asserting a criminal conviction. The draft repeated the widely circulated claim that chief engineer Joseph Erickson was the only person convicted. This is false. What actually happened:

  • A grand jury indicted the owners, the captain, the chief engineer, and federal inspectors.
  • The case was moved to Grand Rapids, Michigan.
  • In February 1916 the defendants were found not guilty, and the court refused extradition

to Illinois, so no criminal trial was ever held in Chicago.

  • The "Erickson was guilty" story comes from a 1933 civil ruling that attributed the disaster to

his use of the ballast tanks. Erickson had died in 1919. The civil claims ended with liability limited to the wreck's salvage value, and the families ultimately received no damages.

Removed from the draft and the caption.

The Eastland was still tied to the dock when it killed 844 people.

Two short sentences. A concrete number and an impossible fact. Nothing withheld that the story does not immediately pay off.

Beat 2 — Context

Purpose: establish why 2,500 people were on a steamship at 7 a.m. on a Saturday.

Western Electric's employees and their families were bound for a company picnic in Michigan City, Indiana. The morning was cool. The gangplank was still down. Nobody had gone anywhere.

Claims: C05, C03, C04

Beat 3 — Development (1): the known flaw

Purpose: show this was not a freak accident.

The Eastland had nearly gone over before — in 1904 with roughly 3,000 aboard, and again in 1912. Its stability was marginal by design.

Claims: C10, C16

Beat 4 — Development (2): the law that made it worse

Purpose: the turn the reader does not expect.

After the Titanic, new safety rules required more lifeboats. To keep its passenger license, the Eastland added boats high on its upper decks. The weight went exactly where the ship could least afford it.

Claims: C11

Beat 5 — Turning point

Purpose: the physical event, plainly told.

Shortly after 7 a.m., the ship rolled onto its side. Water came into the lower decks fast.

Claims: C03, C04

Beat 6 — Why the toll was so high, and why so many lived

Purpose: the cruel geometry that makes this story unique.

The river was about 20 feet deep and the ship lay about 20 feet from the wharf. That shallow, narrow space is why hundreds climbed out alive — and why hundreds below deck had minutes, not hours.

Claims: C06, C07

Beat 7 — Resolution

Purpose: what happened to the dead, the ship, and the people responsible.

844 dead. 590 of them under 25. Twenty-two entire families gone. Indictments followed; the criminal cases ended without anyone held responsible. The ship was raised within weeks, sold to the Navy, renamed USS Wilmette, and served into the 1940s.

Claims: C01, C08, C09, C12, C13, C14

Beat 8 — Ending

Purpose: land the meaning without instructing the reader.

The dock is a short walk from the Loop. The water is 20 feet deep. Most days, nobody looks down.

No call to action. No "follow for more." The final image is a wide panorama of the wreck, and the caption simply stops.

Claims: none (reflection grounded in verified C06, C07)

5. Verification — what the fact-check caught

This is the most important section of the report, because G2 changed the post.

Error 1 — a fabricated name. The draft research named "Paul Steinbrecher" as a defendant whose extradition was refused. No such person is connected to the case in any source. Removed.

Error 2 — a false conviction claim. The draft repeated the widely circulated story that chief engineer Joseph Erickson was the only person convicted. This conflates a 1933 civil ruling with criminal guilt. In fact the defendants were found not guilty in February 1916, the court refused extradition to Illinois, and no criminal trial was ever held in Chicago. Erickson had died in 1919. Corrected, and the caption now states only what is certain.

Also corrected: an unsupported "812" death toll removed; the ship's transfer path to the Navy

corrected; the lifeboat law reframed as one contributing factor, not a cause.

Deliberate omissions: the Titanic/Lusitania comparison (true only in a narrow congressional

form that a caption cannot carry honestly) and the crew death count (sources give 2, 3, or 4). No direct quotations are used, because none could be verified word-for-word with a stable citation.

Result: every factual statement in both captions traces to a claim ID in 03-source-ledger.md.
