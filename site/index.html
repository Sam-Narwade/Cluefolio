The Ship That Never Left the Dock

The SS Eastland disaster. Chicago, July 24, 1915. A ship that never left the wharf, and the 844 people who died anyway.

The story

844 people died on a ship that never went anywhere.

On the morning of July 24, 1915, the excursion steamer Eastland was moored on the Chicago River, loading Western Electric employees and their families for a company picnic. More than 2,500 people came aboard. The gangplank was still down.

Shortly after 7 a.m., the ship rolled onto its side. The river there is about twenty feet deep, and the hull came to rest about twenty feet from the wharf. That shallow, narrow gap is why hundreds climbed out alive — and why hundreds below deck had minutes, not hours.

844 passengers and crew died. 590 were under 25. Twenty-two entire families were wiped out. A grand jury indicted the owners, the captain, the chief engineer, and federal inspectors. The cases ended without a conviction.

The record

What the fact-check changed.

Gate 2 exists because research drifts. In this post it caught two material errors before a single caption was written.

  • A fabricated defendant. The draft named a man who does not appear in any source connected to the case. Removed.
  • A false conviction claim. The widely repeated story that the chief engineer was convicted conflates a 1933 civil ruling with criminal guilt. The defendants were acquitted in February 1916 and no trial was ever held in Chicago. Corrected.

An unsupported death-toll figure was also removed, and the rights audit caught a wrong Library of Congress identifier written from memory. The full trail is in the process record.
