## 1. The story in one paragraph

On the evening of May 6, 1937, the German passenger airship LZ 129 Hindenburg, having completed a transatlantic flight from Frankfurt, Germany, was approaching the mooring mast at Naval Air Station Lakehurst, New Jersey, to land. As the airship slowed and dropped its mooring lines, a fire broke out on the rear upper fin. Within approximately 34 seconds, the entire airship was engulfed in flames and collapsed to the ground. Of the 97 people on board (36 passengers and 61 crew), 35 died (13 passengers, 22 crew), and one additional ground crew member was killed, for a total of 36 fatalities. 62 survived. The disaster was captured on film and photograph, most famously by Sam Shere, whose image shows the airship moments before it collapsed. The cause remains officially undetermined, though the most likely explanation is a leaking hydrogen gas pocket ignited by static electricity.

## 4. The mechanism

Three factors converged. This is the aviation core of the story.

**The lifting gas.** The Hindenburg was filled with hydrogen, which is highly flammable. Helium was available but the U.S. controlled its export, so the German industry continued using hydrogen. The Hindenburg had a history of static discharge issues during flights.

**The approach and docking.** The airship was approaching the mooring mast in rainy conditions. Rain can increase static electricity buildup on the metal skin of the airship. The crew was dropping mooring lines and adjusting position for docking.

**The ignition.** Witnesses observed a bright blue flash on the rear upper fin (the top of the rudder/elevator area) just before the flames spread. This is consistent with a hydrogen gas pocket ignited by a static electric discharge. St. Elmo's Fire was reported in the area that day.

**The rapid spread.** The airship's skin was doped with a highly flammable material (iron oxide and cellulose dope). Once ignited, the fire spread through the hydrogen-filled cells and the skin simultaneously. The entire structure was consumed in approximately 34 seconds.

**Why so many survived.** Despite the rapid destruction, the exit doors and gondola structure provided survivable escape paths. Many survivors jumped from relatively low altitude or escaped through the gondola. The low altitude of the crash (the airship was near the ground when the fire started) meant that the fire spread upward and outward rather than downward into the gondola immediately.

## 5. What was done afterward

A US Commerce Department investigation and a German inquiry both investigated the disaster. Neither could determine the exact source of the ignition. The official finding was that the cause was "conclusive" could not be determined. The German Board of Inquiry (1938) stated that static electricity was the most likely cause but could not prove it. The disaster effectively ended the passenger airship era; no commercial rigid airships were built after 1937.

The Civil Aeronautics Board (CAB) issued its final report in 1939, concluding: "The exact cause of the fire was not determined, but the evidence is consistent with a static discharge igniting hydrogen gas."

## 7. What we still do not know

- The precise point of origin of the first spark within the airship structure.
- Whether the hydrogen leak was from a specific valve, rib, or the gas cell fabric.
- The role of the grounding mast and static discharge chain in the events.
- The detailed second-by-second actions of the crew during the 34-second fire.
