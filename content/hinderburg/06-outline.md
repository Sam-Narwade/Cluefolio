# Narrative Outline — The Airship That Fell From the Sky

--- 

## Beat 1 — Hook

**Purpose:** deliver the contradiction in one breath.

> The Hindenburg was coming in for a landing when it caught fire and fell from the sky.

Two short sentences. A concrete number and an impossible fact. Nothing withheld that the story does not immediately pay off.

Claims: C01, C07

--- 

## Beat 2 — Context

**Purpose:** establish why 97 people were on an airship at the end of its journey.

The Hindenburg had completed a transatlantic flight from Frankfurt, Germany, carrying passengers and crew to the United States. The largest flying machine ever built, it was arriving at Lakehurst, New Jersey, for its first landing of the season. The morning had been rainy, and the approach was complicated by weather.

Claims: C06, C07, C16

--- 

## Beat 3 — Development (1): the ship and the gas

**Purpose:** establish the machine and its lifting gas.

The Hindenburg was a German passenger airship, 804 feet long, filled with hydrogen for lift. It had completed regular transatlantic crossings. Hydrogen provided the lift but carried inherent risk. The skin was treated with a highly flammable dope.

Claims: C06, C11

--- 

## Beat 4 — Development (2): the approach

**Purpose:** the weather and the landing attempt.

The airship approached the mooring mast in rainy conditions. It was a routine landing, the first of the season. The crew was preparing to drop mooring lines and bring the ship to its mast. The Sam Shere photograph captures this moment from the ground.

Claims: C07, C16, C12

--- 

## Beat 5 — Turning point

**Purpose:** the physical event, plainly told.

Fire broke out on the rear upper fin. Within approximately 34 seconds, the entire airship was engulfed in flames and collapsed to the ground. The Sam Shere photograph captures the moment the fire was first observed. The image shows the airship coming in for a landing, then engulfed in flame.

Claims: C08, C12, C13

--- 

## Beat 6 — Why the toll was so high / why so many lived

**Purpose:** the cruel geometry that makes this story unique.

Of the 97 people on board, 35 died and 62 survived. The fire spread rapidly through the hydrogen cells and the flammable skin, but the gondola structure provided survivable exit paths for many. The low altitude of the crash meant some survivors were able to jump or escape. The Sam Shere photograph shows the scale of the event from the ground.

Claims: C04, C05, C14

--- 

## Beat 7 — Resolution

**Purpose:** what happened after the crash.

US and German investigations could not determine the exact cause. The most likely theory is leaking hydrogen gas ignited by static electricity, but no single theory has been proven definitively. The disaster effectively ended the passenger airship era. No commercial rigid airships were built after 1937. The Sam Shere photograph remains the most reproduced image of the disaster, and it is in the public domain.

Claims: C09, C10, C15

--- 

## Beat 8 — Ending

**Purpose:** land the meaning without instructing the reader.

The mooring mast at Lakehurst stands quiet today. The photographs show a sky that went from routine to flame in a split second. The cause may never be proven, but the image endures — and with it, the question of what actually happened in those thirty-four seconds.

No call to action. No "follow for more." The final image is the Sam Shere photograph, and the caption simply stops.

Claims: none (reflection grounded in verified C04, C05, C09)

## Decisions for copy

- The cause (C09) will be phrased as "the most likely explanation" and explicitly qualified as unproven. We do not state "the Hindenburg was caused by" as a definitive fact.
- No direct quotations, because none could be verified word-for-word with a stable citation.
- The death toll (C02, C03, C04) will be stated as clearly distinguished figures: 35 on board, plus 1 on the ground, total 36.
- The Sam Shere photograph is the cover image; it is credited in the caption and in the first comment.
