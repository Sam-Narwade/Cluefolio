The Hindenburg was coming in for a landing when it caught fire and fell from the sky.

Lakehurst, New Jersey · May 6, 1937. The German passenger airship LZ 129 Hindenburg had completed a transatlantic flight from Frankfurt, Germany, and was approaching the mooring mast at Naval Air Station Lakehurst for its first landing of the season. Rain had fallen earlier that afternoon. The airship was flying in a wide circle, waiting for the wind to die down.

More than 90 people were on board as passengers, plus a crew of 61. The airship was filled with hydrogen, not helium — a choice driven by U.S. export controls on helium. Hydrogen provided the lift but carried inherent risk, especially in damp conditions.

The Sam Shere photograph captured the moment the fire was first observed: a bright blue flash on the rear upper fin, moments before the entire structure was engulfed. In approximately thirty-four seconds, the airship was consumed by flame and collapsed to the ground. Of the 97 people on board, 35 died (13 passengers, 22 crew), plus one ground crew member was killed. 62 survived — the largest survival rate for a disaster of its kind.

The cause remains officially undetermined. The most likely explanation, per modern forensic analysis, is a leaking hydrogen gas pocket ignited by static electricity — though the exact source of the spark has never been proven. The airship's skin was treated with a highly flammable dope, which accelerated the spread.

The disaster effectively ended the passenger airship era. No commercial rigid airships were built after 1937. The mooring mast at Lakehurst stands quiet today, and the Sam Shere photograph endures as the most reproduced image of the disaster — a testament to how quickly the sky can go from routine to flame.

Photograph: Sam Shere, via Wikimedia Commons (public domain, 1937).
