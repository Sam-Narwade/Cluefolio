On the evening of May 6, 1937, the German passenger airship LZ 129 Hindenburg was approaching the mooring mast at Naval Air Station Lakehurst, New Jersey, to land. The airship had completed a transatlantic flight from Frankfurt, Germany, and was executing a routine docking maneuver. Rain had fallen earlier that afternoon, and the ship was flying in a wide circle, waiting for conditions to improve.

More than 90 people were on board as passengers, plus a crew of 61. The airship was the largest flying machine ever built, and it was filled with hydrogen — not helium, which was available but controlled by the United States. The hydrogen provided lift but carried inherent risk, especially in damp conditions.

Witnesses on the ground observed a bright blue flash on the rear upper fin just before the flames broke out. The Sam Shere photograph captured this moment from the ground, showing the airship coming in for a landing, then moments later fully engulfed in fire. Within approximately thirty-four seconds, the entire airship was consumed by flame and collapsed to the ground. The fire spread with extraordinary speed through the hydrogen-filled cells and the flammable skin dope.

Of the 97 people on board, 35 died (13 passengers, 22 crew), plus one additional ground crew member was killed, for a total of 36 fatalities. 62 survived. The disaster effectively ended the passenger airship era; no commercial rigid airships were built after 1937.

The cause remains officially undetermined. The most likely explanation, per modern forensic analysis, is a leaking hydrogen gas pocket ignited by static electricity — though the exact source of the spark has never been proven. The airship's skin was doped with a highly flammable material, which accelerated the spread of the fire.

The Sam Shere photograph, taken from the ground at Lakehurst, shows the airship moments before it fell from the sky. It is the most published image of the disaster and is in the public domain.

Photograph: Sam Shere, via Wikimedia Commons (public domain, 1937).
