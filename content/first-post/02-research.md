## 1. The story in one paragraph

On the morning of July 24, 1915, roughly 2,500 Western Electric employees and their families
boarded the excursion steamer *Eastland* at a wharf on the Chicago River for a company picnic in
Michigan City, Indiana. The ship had a documented history of severe listing. New lifeboat
requirements, added after the *Titanic*, had placed additional weight high on an already top-heavy
hull. Shortly after 7 a.m., with the gangplank still down and the ship still tied to the dock, the
*Eastland* rolled onto its side. Because the river was only about 20 feet deep and the ship lay only
about 20 feet from the wharf, hundreds were able to escape across the exposed hull — but 844 people
died, most of them below decks. Prosecutions followed and failed. The ship was raised, sold to the
Navy, renamed USS *Wilmette*, and served into the 1940s.

## 4. The mechanism

Three factors combined. This is the engineering core of the story.

**Design and history.** The *Eastland* was built narrow and fast, which tends to make a hull
top-heavy. Its stability was marginal from the start, and it had a documented record of severe
listing — in 1904, 1906, and 1912 — before the disaster (C10, C12).

**Added top-weight.** After the *Titanic*, the Seamen's Act of 1915 required more lifeboats and
rafts. To keep its licensed passenger capacity, the *Eastland*'s operators added boats high on the
superstructure. That weight sat above the ship's center of gravity and worsened the very problem
that had nearly sunk the ship before (C11).

**The morning it happened.** Passengers gathered on the side of the ship away from the river as it
listed and corrected. When it rolled, the open lower decks and portholes allowed water in quickly,
and the ship went onto its side within a short time (C03).

**Why so many died, and why so many lived.** The *Eastland* settled into roughly 20 feet of water
about 20 feet from the wharf. The shallow water and the proximity of the dock saved hundreds, who
climbed out across the exposed hull. It also meant the lower decks flooded within minutes, killing
the people trapped there — which is why the death toll was so high despite the ship never leaving
the dock (C04, C05, C06).

## 5. What was done afterward

A coroner's inquest and a grand jury investigated. The grand jury indicted the ship's owners, the
captain, the chief engineer, and federal inspectors. The case was moved to Grand Rapids, Michigan,
where the defendants were found not guilty in February 1916, and the court refused extradition to
Illinois — so no criminal trial was ever held in Chicago. No one was criminally convicted. The civil
claims dragged on for years and ended with liability limited to the wreck's salvage value; the
families ultimately received no damages (C12). The ship itself was raised on August 14, 1915, sold,
and later commissioned as the Navy's USS *Wilmette*, serving into the 1940s before being scrapped
(C13, C14).

## 7. What we still do not know

- The precise second-by-second sequence aboard the lower decks is not recoverable.
- Sources differ on the exact time the ship rolled; we say "shortly after 7 a.m." to stay safe.
- The number of crew among the dead is genuinely inconsistent across reputable sources.

## 9. Notes on sourcing quality

- NARA and the Library of Congress provide authoritative primary imagery.
- The congressional record provides the age and family statistics, which is a strong source tier.
- Popular retellings of the disaster frequently repeat the unfounded "never should have been built"
  line and the "more than the *Titanic*" comparison. Neither will appear unqualified.
- Wikipedia was used only as a map to better sources, never as a final citation.
